🩹 Achilles Readiness — Holiday Health Plan

Gerlos 2026 · Aug 31–Sep 8 · Built for the body actually trained, not the plan that was prescribed. Last updated Aug 30, 2026.

VERDICT: Ready to hike, not reckless

Risk: MODERATE — front-loaded into the first half of the trip

The tendon is in better shape than the plan assumed, and the gear is optimal for this specific injury (heel lifts + high-cut boots + poles). The one unbuilt capacity is steep descent on consecutive days — and exactly one day outpaces what was actually trained: Olpererhütte on Day 4.

📊 The Honest Picture — What Was Built vs Not

Built this summer (Jul–Aug):

  • Consistent aerobic base — 6-round run-walk 2×/week, always ≥48h between runs
  • ~7 km unloaded walks 1–2×/week
  • 2 informal loaded walks (7–12 km, ~7 kg pack) — at least one on trail, in the boots + heel lifts + poles
  • Indoor bouldering 1×/week (bonus calf + proprioception load)
  • No graded hike sims · no controlled descent practice · no continuous jog · no back-to-back test
The key signal: tendon irritability decreased all summer — same-day pain in early June became same-day stiffness by August, and walks only ever cause hours-long same-day stiffness. No next-morning stiffness all summer.

⚠️ The One Day That Outpaces the Build

Olpererhütte is your hardest hike (876m ascent and 876m descent) on the third consecutive hiking day. For calibration: this is far milder than the block that caused the original injury — that was 4–5 consecutive days ending in an ~850m ↑↓ / 18km day that also included running up a steep switchback slope and a ~100m run to a waterfall. Olperer is a normal graded hike, but with no back-to-back test and no descent practice this summer, it's still the single highest-risk day. Your gear makes it doable, not unconditional.

The rule: morning of Day 4, any Achilles stiffness → swap Olperer for a lift day (Penken/Isskogel) and push Olperer to Day 7. Decide at the morning, not at the trailhead.
Cold-start caution: the 6:30am departure is the worst time to load a tendon. 10-min warm-up walk before any steep section. Descend slowly — poles planted ahead on every step, not just the steep bits.

🔒 Non-Negotiables

  • Poles on every descent (carry from Day 1, not "when it gets steep")
  • Pack ≤7 kg — hip belt takes the load. Self-lightens as you drink/eat: correct pattern
  • Downhill cue everywhere: short steps, bent knees, poles ahead
  • Days 1–3 easy — use lifts to cut vertical gain; don't open with the hardest day
  • Light day around Day 3–4 (never did a back-to-back test — give the tendon a catch-up day)
  • No heavy rehab on top of hiking days — the trail is the load

🥾 Gear — Optimal for This Injury

ItemWhat it does
Thin sloped heel liftsPlantarflex the ankle → shorten tendon excursion → directly cut eccentric descent load. "Little thickness" is correct.
High-cut bootsMildly restrict dorsiflexion → cap tendon stretch on descents. Pairs with the lifts.
Trekking poles~25% less descent force. You've used them on 2 prior trips — a habit, not a new skill.

Lifts + boots + poles = maximal non-surgical offload for descents. Trail-tested on a 7–12 km hike in August. Wear the lifts in both boots every hiking day; don't stack extra padding on top.

🏋️ Rehab During the Trip (Alfredson heel drops)

Pause the daily Alfredson during the holiday. The descents are the eccentric calf load — that's exactly what the Alfredson heel-drop mimics. Doing heavy eccentric heel drops on top of 5–6h hikes (with 876m descents) is double-loading the tendon — the same overload pattern that caused the original injury.

  • Evening after hiking: light static calf stretch only — straight-knee (gastrocnemius) + bent-knee (soleus), 2×30s, gentle. This is recovery, not load.
  • Full rest day (Shabbat) + quiet tendon: optional light session — reduced volume, bodyweight only, no heavy.
  • After you return: resume the full Mon/Wed Alfredson + posterior-chain routine. Hiking is a different load signature and doesn't replace the structured tendon progression long-term.
